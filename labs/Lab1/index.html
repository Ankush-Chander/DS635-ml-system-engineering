Lab 1 — The Matmul Ladder on Your Machine

In class, we ran the ladder on one specific laptop. This lab is about rebuilding the curve on your own hardware and explaining every place it differs.

  • Time: ~2 hours
  • Machines: any Linux CPU box (no GPU needed — the GPU rungs skip automatically)
  • Work in pairs, submit individually.

The one rule

Every experiment below follows the same loop:

Predict → Measure → Explain.

Write your prediction down before running the command. A wrong prediction followed by a correct explanation earns full marks. A correct number with no explanation earns almost none — this is a systems course, not a benchmarking contest.

Part 0 — Setup (10 min)

git clone git@github.com:Ankush-Chander/DS635-ml-system-engineering.git
cd DS635-ml-system-engineering/code/ai_acceleration
make data && make && make bench && make chart

You need: gcc, make, python3 with numpy + matplotlib, and valgrind (for Part 2 — check with valgrind --version).

Sanity checks before moving on:

  • Every line in results/results.csv ends in PASS. A FAIL means an "optimization" broke the math — that's a bug, not a speedup.
  • rung7/rung8 were skipped (no GPU) — expected, not an error.
  • Keep results/ladder.png — you'll submit it.

Also record your machine's identity — you'll need every one of these numbers later:

lscpu | grep -E "Model name|^CPU\(s\)|Core\(s\)|Thread|MHz"
lscpu | grep -i cache        # L1d, L2, L3 sizes
nproc

Patience point

The naive C rung at N=2048 takes ~1–2 minutes on a lab machine. That slowness is the data point.

Part 2 — Cache detective (25 min)

The lecture claimed the naive→reorder speedup is entirely a cache-line story. Don't believe it — audit it.

Predict first. At N=1024, using the two facts cache line = 64 B = 16 floats and the inner loop reads A along a row and B down a column:

  • Predicted L1d misses, naive i-j-k: ______ (hint: roughly one per B access)
  • Predicted L1d misses, reordered i-k-j: ______ (hint: one per cache line)

Now measure (cachegrind simulates the cache — no sudo needed, but ~50× slower; the naive run takes ~5–10 min, start it and read ahead):

for b in rung2_c_naive rung3_c_reorder; do
  echo "== $b =="
  valgrind --tool=cachegrind --cache-sim=yes ./build/$b 1024 1 2>&1 \
    | grep -E "D  refs|D1  miss|LLd miss"
done
Predicted Measured Ratio measured/predicted
Naive D1 misses
Reordered D1 misses

💬 Q2.1 — The naive run's read miss rate comes out to almost exactly 50%. The inner loop makes exactly two reads per iteration. Which one misses, which one hits, and why?

💬 Q2.2 — LLd (last-level) misses are nearly identical for both versions, even though one is 9× faster. What are these misses (name the category), and what does their equality tell you about where in the hierarchy the 9× was won?

Do not report cachegrind's runtime as a performance number

Simulation overhead flattens the real difference. Harness for time, cachegrind for why.

Part 3 — Find your memory wall (25 min)

In class, the SIMD rung collapsed from 31.5 to 14.2 GFLOP/s between N=1024 and N=2048 — because matrix B (4N² bytes) stopped fitting in that laptop's 16 MB L3. Your L3 is a different size, so your wall is in a different place.

Predict first. From your recorded L3 size, solve 4N² = L3 for N: the wall should appear once N clearly exceeds N* = ______.

Measure. Generate the extra sizes, then sweep (any N works as long as its data exists):

python3 common/gen_inputs.py --n 512 1536 3072
for n in 512 1024 1536 2048 3072 4096; do ./build/rung4_c_simd $n 3; done

Tabulate GFLOP/s vs N and mark where the cliff starts.

💬 Q3.1 — Where did your cliff land relative to your predicted N*? If they disagree, consider: is L3 shared or per-core-complex on your CPU? Does the working set include more than just B?

💬 Q3.2 — Same binary, same flags, same algorithm at every N — in one sentence, what changed across the cliff?

Part 4 — Tiling: the sweep and the trap (25 min)

4a. Find your cache's sweet spot

The tiled rung's T×T working set must fit in cache: roughly 3 × T² × 4 bytes for the three tiles in play. Predict your best T from your L1d and L2 sizes, then sweep:

for t in 32 64 128 256; do
  make clean && make T=$t
  ./build/rung5_c_tiled 2048 3
done
T 3·T²·4 bytes Fits in… GFLOP/s
32 12 KB
64 48 KB
128 192 KB
256 768 KB

💬 Q4.1 — Explain your winning T from your cache sizes. Why does T=256 (or your largest losing T) fall off, even though bigger tiles mean more reuse per byte?

4b. The three-line bounds check that costs 3×

Open rung5_c_tiled.c, find the innermost loop, and change it to:

for (int j = jj; j < jj + T && j < n; j++)

Rebuild with your best T and re-run at N=2048. Then check what the compiler actually emitted, before vs after (revert the edit for the "before" count):

gcc -O3 -march=native -ffast-math -S -o - rung5_c_tiled.c | grep -c ymm

💬 Q4.2 — Report GFLOP/s and the ymm instruction count with and without the guard. The guard is semantically harmless here (N % T == 0, so j < n is always true). Why does the compiler give up vectorizing anyway — what would it have to prove, and can it?

Revert your edit when done (git checkout rung5_c_tiled.c).

Stretch (optional, +4)

Pick one:

  1. Percent of peak. Compute your CPU's theoretical peak: cores × clock (GHz) × SIMD lanes × 2 (FMA = mul+add) × FMA units per core (most AVX2 desktops: 8 lanes, 2 FMA units → 32 FLOPs/cycle/core). What fraction of peak does your best OpenMP number reach? What fraction does NumPy reach? List two things BLAS does that our ladder still doesn't.
  2. Prove tiling needs a memory problem. Rebuild the tiled rung with the scalar flags (-O2 -fno-tree-vectorize -fno-tree-slp-vectorize) and show tiling gains ≈ nothing over the scalar reordered rung. One paragraph: why is this the roofline model in miniature?
  3. Break the harness (on purpose). Change -ffast-math to nothing in the FAST flags, rebuild the SIMD rung, and measure. How much of the SIMD speedup depended on the compiler being allowed to reorder float additions — and why does IEEE-754 forbid it by default?
