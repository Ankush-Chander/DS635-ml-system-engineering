Lab 13/14 — Decoding Strategies, Measured

Lab thesis: a decoding strategy is a choice about a distribution. This lab makes you measure the distribution a model emits and what each strategy does to it — greedy's determinism, temperature's reshaping, top-k vs top-p's truncation — so the lecture's rules become numbers you produced.

This is the experimental half of Lecture 13/14 — Decoding strategies. The lecture derived each strategy from the shape of the next-token distribution. Here you produce that distribution on a model you control and confirm the relationships the lecture claims.

Runs on Google Colab (free) or your own machine — no GPU required. The model is gpt2 (124M); the CPU is enough.

Open the notebook in Colab · read it on this site · download from GitHub

What this is not

It is not the lecture's companion notebook re-run. That one showed you greedy loops and top-p adapts. Here you predict every relationship before you look, then explain the mechanism:

The lecture showed This lab makes you produce
the model emits a distribution over ~50k tokens the top-50 mass, and the long tail it leaves
greedy is deterministic two greedy runs, byte-identical, and greedy's first token = the argmax
temperature reshapes the distribution P(top token) falling as T rises, and diversity rising with it
top-p adapts, top-k does not the nucleus size on a peaked vs a flat prompt, against top-k's fixed count
beam finds a higher-probability sequence whether it actually does on your run — and why a heuristic may not

What you need

  • Python 3.10+, torch, transformers. First run downloads gpt2 (~500 MB), then it is cached.
  • No GPU, no lab hardware. Colab's free CPU runtime is enough.

What you do

Set ROLL_NUMBER and NAME first. The sampling in Part 2 is seeded from your roll number, so your diversity numbers are yours — that is what the plagiarism check reads.

Part 1 — The distribution and greedy (25 marks)

Take one forward pass and read the next-token distribution. Report the top-50 probability mass.

Then run greedy decoding twice and show that the two outputs are identical, and that greedy's first token is the argmax of the distribution.

Predict then explain: why greedy is deterministic while sampling is not, and what the top-50 mass says about the tail Part 3 exists to cut.

Part 2 — Temperature (25 marks)

On the same logits, sweep the temperature T and record P(top token) at each. Separately, sample many continuations at low, medium and high T and record the distinct-token ratio at each.

You are marked on establishing these relationships between your own numbers:

Relationship Why it holds
P(top token) falls as T rises softmax(l/T) shrinks the top logit's lead
distinct-token ratio rises as T rises a flatter distribution spreads the draws

One paragraph: using P_i / P_j = exp((l_i − l_j)/T), why T flattens the distribution, and what happens as T → 0.

Part 3 — Truncation: top-k vs top-p (20 marks)

On a peaked prompt (one obvious next word) and a flat prompt (many options), record the nucleus size (top-p) for each, and top-k's count for each.

Show that:

  • nucleus keeps fewer tokens on the peaked prompt than on the flat one — it adapts,
  • top-k keeps the same count on both — it does not,
  • the nucleus reaches probability p while dropping a real tail (kept mass ≥ p, set ≪ vocabulary).

One paragraph: the failure each truncation fixes, and the failure each still has.

Part 4 — Beam vs greedy (30 marks, almost all rubric)

Fold each output's tokens back through the model and sum their log-probabilities. Compare beam search's total against greedy's.

The paragraph that carries this part: did beam beat greedy on your run? Beam is not exhaustive — it prunes. Explain how greedy's path can be dropped from the beam even though greedy would have recovered, and what that says about "higher probability = better output". If beam won, say what it found that greedy could not see.

This part is deliberately not auto-marked: beam does not reliably beat greedy, and reporting that honestly is the point.

Finally, choose a decoding strategy for code generation, a chatbot reply, and machine translation, one line each, tied to a number you measured.

What you submit

Two files. The JSON carries your measurements; the notebook carries your reasoning.

  1. submission_lab13_14_<roll>.json — written by the notebook's final cell. Set ROLL_NUMBER and NAME first.
  2. The executed notebook — outputs intact, every 📝 prediction and explanation cell filled in.

Run every cell top to bottom before exporting.

How it is marked

Automatic (from your JSON) — each check tests a relationship, never a magnitude against a reference:

  • greedy run 1 == run 2, and greedy's first token == argmax
  • P(top token) falls monotonically as T rises
  • distinct-token ratio rises with T
  • nucleus(peaked) < nucleus(flat), while top-k is fixed across both
  • top-p kept mass ≥ p, and the nucleus is a proper subset of the vocabulary

Rubric (from your written cells) — predictions marked for being made, explanations for naming the mechanism: argmax determinism, the temperature ratio, the long tail, the adaptive nucleus, beam pruning. The heaviest single item is Part 4's paragraph on why beam can lose to greedy.

40 automatic + 60 rubric, out of 100. The automatic half is marked by code/decoding/grade_lab13_14.py from the JSON your notebook's export cell writes.

An honest surprising measurement, correctly explained, scores higher than a tidy expected one. If beam lost to greedy on your run, say so and explain the pruning.

Academic integrity

The sampling in Part 2 is seeded from your roll number, so two honest submissions never share a diversity vector. Submissions sharing that vector are treated as copied. Discussing mechanisms is encouraged; sharing measurement files is not.
