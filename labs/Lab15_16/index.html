Lab 15/16 — Speculative Decoding, Measured

Lab thesis: speculative decoding is a sampling rule, not a model trick — a small drafter proposes, a large target verifies in one pass, and a residual correction keeps the output exactly the target's distribution. This lab makes you build that rule yourself: first on dummy distributions where correctness is provable, then as a real two-model stitch.

This is the experimental half of Lecture 15/16 — Speculative decoding. The lecture proved the guarantee on paper; here you implement it and confirm the relationships it claims.

Runs on Google Colab (free) or your own machine — no GPU required. The real stitch uses gpt2 (target) drafted by distilgpt2 — a same-tokenizer pair, both small enough for CPU.

Open the notebook in Colab · read it on this site · download from GitHub

The two silent failures this lab is built around

  • The shared tokenizer. The accept test compares p(x) and q(x) for the same integer x; a mismatched tokenizer is not an error, it is acceptance near zero. That is why the stitch works within a model family (gpt2 ← distilgpt2) but not across.
  • The residual. On a reject you must resample from norm(max(0, p − q)), not from p. Both have the identical acceptance rate; only a distribution test tells them apart. Part 2 puts that on trial.

What you need

  • Python 3.10+, numpy, torch, transformers. First run downloads gpt2 + distilgpt2 (~0.5 GB total), then cached.
  • No GPU, no lab hardware. Colab's free CPU runtime is enough.

What you do

Set ROLL_NUMBER and NAME first. The dummy sampling is seeded from your roll number, so your numbers are yours — that is what the plagiarism check reads. You implement the cells marked # TODO; the harnesses and dummy data are given.

Part 1 — The acceptance branch (15 marks)

Implement is_accepted — accept a draft x with probability min(1, p[x] / q[x]). The harness measures the empirical accept rate at each dummy position; it must track min(1, p/q).

Explain: why the probability is capped at 1, and why over-proposing (q > p) is throttled.

Part 2 — Rejection sampling: the residual (30 marks)

Implement residual = norm(max(0, p − q)) and sample_on_reject. The harness runs the single-position emitter many times and compares the emitted distribution to p — and runs a broken control that resamples from p.

Show that the correct version's emitted distribution matches p (small total-variation distance) while the broken one does not — at an identical acceptance rate.

The paragraph that carries this part: why the residual and not p, and why only a distribution test (not a latency or acceptance dashboard) catches the difference.

Part 3 — Vectorised verification (15 marks)

Implement verify_vectorised — the k ratio tests as array ops, the cut at the first reject sequential. The harness checks it agrees with a scalar reference over thousands of random draws.

Explain: what is embarrassingly parallel and what is not, and how that mirrors "verification is a prefill".

Part 4 — The real stitch: gpt2 ← distilgpt2 (40 marks)

Implement speculative_greedy — draft k tokens from distilgpt2, verify them in one gpt2 pass, accept while the target's argmax matches, emit the target's token at the first mismatch (or the bonus if all accept).

You are marked on establishing these relationships between your own numbers:

Relationship Why it holds
speculative-greedy output == plain greedy, token for token greedy speculation is lossless by construction
target forward passes < tokens generated one pass verifies k+1 positions
every block yields between 1 and k+1 tokens reject still emits the target's own token

The write-up: on your CPU, is speculative decoding actually faster in wall-clock? Fewer target passes, yet the drafter runs its own passes — reconcile the two, and say why a bad drafter costs speed but never accuracy.

What you submit

Two files. The JSON carries your measurements; the notebook carries your reasoning.

  1. submission_lab15_16_<roll>.json — written by the notebook's final cell. Set ROLL_NUMBER and NAME first.
  2. The executed notebook — outputs intact, every 📝 prediction and explanation cell filled in.

How it is marked

Automatic (from your JSON) — each check tests a relationship, never a magnitude against a reference:

  • accept rate tracks min(1, p/q) at each position
  • the emitted distribution matches p (small TV); the broken control does not
  • vectorised verification agrees with the scalar cut
  • speculative-greedy output == plain greedy; target passes < tokens; accepted-run lengths in [0, k]

Rubric (from your written cells) — explanations marked for naming the mechanism: the ratio test, the residual, the silent bug, the prefill, acceptance-vs-speedup. The heaviest items are Part 2's residual paragraph and Part 4's acceptance-is-not-speedup write-up.

40 automatic + 60 rubric, out of 100. The automatic half is marked by code/spec_decode/grade_lab15_16.py from the JSON your notebook writes.

An honest surprising measurement, correctly explained, scores higher than a tidy expected one. If speculative decoding was slower than plain greedy on your CPU — near-perfect drafting and still slower — say so and explain why the drafter's own passes cost more than they saved.

Academic integrity

The dummy sampling is seeded from your roll number, so two honest submissions never share a measurement vector. Submissions sharing that vector are treated as copied. Discussing mechanisms is encouraged; sharing measurement files is not.
